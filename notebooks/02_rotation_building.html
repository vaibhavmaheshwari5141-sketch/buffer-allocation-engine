02 · Rotation building — chains, turns, minimum turnaround and buffer¶

Buffer Delay Allocation Engine · Phase 2 (data contract v1.1)

This notebook turns the 1.64 million AA flights of the Phase 1 base table into aircraft chains, builds the turns between consecutive legs, estimates the minimum turnaround time (MTT), derives the planned buffer, and tests the premise the whole project rests on: does planned buffer absorb delay?

Inputs: interim/aa_base_2025_2026.parquet, reference/airports_tz.csv. Outputs (all under processed/): rotations.parquet (one row per flight), rotation_turns.parquet, rotation_breaks.parquet, mtt_sensitivity.parquet, plus small CSVs. Built by bufferengine.pipeline.build_rotations; the analysis functions are in bufferengine.pipeline.rotation_audit.

Provenance. Flights, delays and LateAircraftDelay are real BTS data. Chain links, UTC times, ground times and buffer are derived. MTT is an assumption (estimated from data, with sensitivity), so planned_buffer_min is an assumption-dependent derived field. Nothing here is synthetic.

Rules.

  1. Chains use operated flights only. Cancelled and diverted flights stay in the aircraft's sequence but break the chain.
  2. Per aircraft, legs are ordered by scheduled UTC departure. Consecutive legs link only if previous Dest = next Origin and the next scheduled departure is not before the previous scheduled arrival. A pair failing the second test is a tail swap (BTS reports the aircraft that actually flew): the chain breaks and the pair is counted.
  3. Operating day: a new operating day starts when scheduled ground time exceeds 300 minutes (the aircraft rests; the 240–300 minute bucket is the valley between daytime turns and overnight rests in the data). A leg crossing midnight, and an early leg following a short turn after it, stay in the same operating day.
  4. A segment is a run of linked operated legs. Segments of 2+ legs are usable; single-leg segments are kept with usable = false.
  5. Splits: train = 2025-01 to 2025-09, validate = 2025-10 to 2025-12, test = 2026-01 to 2026-08. MTT is estimated from train turns only.

3. Scheduled ground time, estimated MTT and planned buffer¶

MTT is an assumption, estimated from train data only. When the inbound flight arrived late and the outbound flight left late, the aircraft was probably turned as fast as possible, so the low tail of the actual ground time on those turns approximates the minimum. Estimate = the 10th percentile of actual ground time per station and time-of-day band (morning 05–09, midday 10–14, afternoon 15–19, night 20–04, local time of the outbound departure), using only train turns with a valid (positive) ground time. Fallback when a cell has fewer than 50 such turns: station class (high_volume = at least 20 train turns/day, 13 stations) by band, then one global value. Floor 25 min (below the 1st percentile of observed pressured turns, about 32; it never binds, the lowest raw estimate is 27), cap 60 min (above every high-volume and mainland estimate, which top out around 55; it binds for HNL and one ANC cell, see below). planned_buffer_min = max(0, scheduled ground − MTT).

No validate or test turn is used to estimate MTT (enforced by a unit test).

What this shows. Across the 1,045,807 turns the median scheduled ground time is 60 min and the median estimated MTT is 47 min, so the median planned buffer is only 14 min (mean 26). 7.7% of turns have zero planned buffer (6.4% in train, 9.4% in test) and 50.9% have under 15 minutes; 6.9% are scheduled below MTT.

MTT estimates (assumption). Global value 42 min from 127,461 pressured train turns (27% of train turns). By class: 46-48 min at high-volume stations and 37-39 min elsewhere, a plausible narrowbody turn. 97.2% of turns use a station-by-band estimate (the rest the class fallback). High-volume stations have more zero-buffer turns (8-11%) than other stations (2-4%).

The cap binds in one place. For HNL (three cells, raw 10th percentile of 71-87 min) and ANC at night (60 min) the raw estimate is at or above the 60-minute cap, so MTT is capped for 3,736 turns (0.36%), probably because long Pacific flights need longer turns. For those turns MTT is understated and planned buffer overstated. The floor never binds (lowest raw estimate 27 min).

Sensitivity. Moving the percentile from p05 to p20 moves the median MTT from 44 to 51 min and the zero-buffer share from 5.7% to 13.9% (under 15 min: 39.6% to 60.8%; median buffer 17 to 11 min). The default p10 is in the middle: the 10th percentile of "time actually needed when pressed" is a conservative but not extreme definition of the minimum.

Reading the result honestly. The premise holds, but the straight-line slope is not what shows it.

  • Slopes (train, 95% CI): 1.09 [1.04, 1.14] at zero buffer, 1.10 [1.08, 1.12] at 1-15, 1.06 [1.03, 1.08] at 16-30, 0.95 [0.91, 0.99] at 31-60 and 0.44 [0.41, 0.47] at 60+. The slope falls as buffer rises, but only beyond ~30 minutes of buffer; below that it is flat at about 1. At zero buffer it is above 1 because turns scheduled below MTT also lose the shortfall (the intercept is +29 min).
  • Absorption table (the clearer view). For the same inbound delay, outbound delay falls steeply with buffer. For a 16-30 minute inbound delay (mean 22): outbound departure delay 52 min at zero buffer, 31 at 1-15, 18 at 16-30, 9 at 31-60 and the share leaving 15+ minutes late falls from 97% to 76%, 39%, 17%. For 31-60 minutes inbound (mean 43): 76, 54, 42, 21, 12 min. For more than 60 minutes inbound (mean 110-130): 152, 137, 115, 91, 42 min. Roughly one minute of delay is absorbed per extra minute of buffer until the buffer exceeds the inbound delay, after which more buffer adds little (diminishing returns).
  • Composition check. Within high-volume stations and within other stations the pattern is the same (high-volume slopes 1.13, 1.10, 1.03, 0.93, 0.40).

Caveats. Buffer is not randomly assigned; stations, aircraft and times of day differ, so this is strong association rather than proof of causation. Bucket boundaries depend on the MTT assumption. Inbound delay is comparable across buckets (e.g. 22.1-22.5 min in the 16-30 band), which makes the comparison like-for-like on the inbound side.

What this shows. The claim "early delays cause more downstream damage" is supported by exposure, not by a higher pass-through per turn.

  • Slope by turn index shows no trend (0.85, 0.81, 0.82, 0.95, 0.83): a late turn passes delay on about as much as an early one.
  • Slope by hour is actually lower in the morning (0.66-0.84 at 07:00-11:00, probably because morning buffers are longer: median 21 min vs 15-16 in the afternoon at high-volume stations) and about 0.9 from 17:00 to 19:00.
  • Exposure: the mean number of legs still to fly after the turn is 3.1-4.0 for turns before 11:00 and 1.6-1.9 between 17:00 and 21:59. A delay minute passed on at a morning turn can hit 3-4 more legs, one in the evening only about 1.6.

So early-day buffer matters because of what is downstream; the simulator must model that chain effect explicitly rather than rely on a per-turn slope. Note leg_index_in_segment and n_downstream_legs come from the realised chain: a segment cut short by a cancellation or swap understates the planned number of downstream legs.

8. Conclusion¶

Usable for Phases 3 to 6¶

  • 1,408,848 legs in 363,041 usable segments and 1,045,807 turns (train 466,562, validate 158,292, test 420,953), about 1,700 turns per day: a size an exact solver can handle day by day.
  • A defensible MTT and planned buffer, with a sensitivity table for p05 / p10 / p20, estimated from train data only.
  • Observed pass-through by buffer level: the evidence that buffer absorbs delay (section 4), to calibrate and validate the simulator.
  • Storm and high-cancellation flags to run backtests with and without disrupted days.
  • A complete reconciliation: every base flight is in one category, enforced by a test.

Limitations and risks¶

  1. Planned rotation is assumed to equal the operated rotation. BTS gives the aircraft that actually flew. 33,461 pairs (3.1% of candidate pairs) show the plan and the operation disagree, and they are broken, not repaired. Segments are therefore shorter than the planned rotations and n_downstream_legs is a lower bound on the true downstream exposure.
  2. MTT is an assumption. The zero-buffer share moves between 5.7% and 13.9% across p05-p20, and every buffer figure shifts with it. The optimiser results must be shown with that sensitivity.
  3. Buffer is already thin and the budget is small. The median planned buffer is 14 min and half of all turns have under 15. The network already holds about 45,000 buffer minutes per day (1,720 turns x 26 min on average); a budget of 500 extra minutes per day is about 1% of that, so the optimiser is really choosing where to add a small increment. Absorption saturates once buffer exceeds the likely inbound delay, so the marginal value of a minute is highest on short buffers that precede delay-prone arrivals.
  4. Leakage. leg_index_in_segment, n_legs, n_downstream_legs, segment_id, break_reason_* and n_segments depend on cancellations and swaps that happen on the day, so they must not be prediction features until the leakage review before modelling.
  5. Discarded or excluded: 191,021 single-leg-segment flights, 35,245 cancelled and 5,957 diverted flights, and any pair separated by a rest of more than 300 minutes (overnight): delay does not carry across those, and nothing is lost by the rule since the next day starts a new operating day.
  6. Disrupted days distort averages (27 high-cancellation days, five of them the storm). Report with and without.
  7. Pass-through is association. Buffer is not randomly assigned. The simulator should be validated against observed outcomes, not assumed causal.
