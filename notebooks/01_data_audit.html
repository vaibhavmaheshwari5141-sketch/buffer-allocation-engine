01 · Data audit — AA on-time data, Jan 2025 – Aug 2026¶

Buffer Delay Allocation Engine · Phase 1

This notebook audits the AA base table built from the BTS Reporting Carrier On-Time Performance monthly files, before any rotation building or modelling. It answers: is the data complete and clean enough, and can aircraft rotations be reconstructed from it reliably?

  • Input: interim/aa_base_2025_2026.parquet (from bufferengine.pipeline.build_base) and reference/airports_tz.csv (from bufferengine.pipeline.reference), both under DATA_ROOT.
  • Logic: all checks are reusable functions in src/bufferengine/pipeline/audit.py; this notebook only calls and presents them.
  • Runs locally (repo data/ folder) or in Databricks (set DATA_ROOT to the Volume, done automatically below).

All numbers are computed from real BTS data. Nothing here is synthetic or assumed.

What this shows. Missing values follow the BTS reporting rules, not random gaps:

  • Departure fields are missing for 2.1% of rows, all of them cancelled flights (about 3% of cancelled flights still have a departure time); arrival and ArrDelay fields for 2.5% (cancelled plus diverted). Operated flights have no missing values in any timing field.
  • The five delay-cause columns are 74.8% missing overall. The pattern table confirms they are populated exactly for late arrivals: of 414,031 operated flights with ArrDelay >= 15, 100% have causes populated and in 100% of them the five causes add up to ArrDelay (within 0.5 min); of the 1,185,838 operated flights below 15 minutes, none do. A missing cause therefore means "not a late arrival", not "unknown".
  • CancellationCode is populated only for cancelled flights (97.85% missing overall, as expected).

What this shows. Of operated flights, 25.71% arrived 15+ minutes late in 2025 (on-time 74.29%) and 26.13% in Jan–Aug 2026. The rate is strongly seasonal: 19% in January 2025, peaking at 35.0% in July 2025 and 33.1% in July 2026.

Comparison with the 76.43% on-time figure in the D1 charter (Cirium, 2025). Our 2025 on-time rate is 74.29%, i.e. 2.1 points lower, or 72.70% if cancelled and diverted flights are counted as not on time. We do not force a match. Likely reasons (not verified against the Cirium report):

  1. Scope. BTS has domestic flights only; a Cirium figure for "American Airlines" probably includes international flying, which can have a different on-time rate and would shift the average.
  2. Definition and denominator. Both use a 15-minute threshold, but how cancellations, diversions and the "within 14 minutes" boundary are treated can differ by a point or more (the two columns above differ by 1.6 points for 2025).
  3. Different base. BTS counts every scheduled domestic mainline flight; commercial reports may exclude some flights or use a different airline definition.

For this project the BTS definition is the reference: the model target is ArrDel15 on BTS operated flights. The D1 charter figure should be cited as an external benchmark, not a reconciliation target.

What this shows. There are 478,938 tail-days (one aircraft on one date). Aircraft fly 3–4 legs on a typical day (median 3, most common 4, rarely more than 8), and 89% of tail-days have 2+ legs, so chains are long enough for propagation to matter.

Continuity. Among consecutive scheduled legs (1.16 million pairs), 95.7% are continuous (previous destination = next origin). Breaks: 2.9% involve a cancelled leg and 0.02% a diversion. Once cancelled legs are removed, 98.50% of consecutive operated pairs are continuous, 1.48% are unexplained and 0.02% follow a diversion. Aircraft are also positioned for the next morning: 97.3% of consecutive-day pairs end where the next day starts.

Usable tail-days (Phase 2 definition). Strict: 2+ legs, none cancelled or diverted, all pairs continuous: 387,710 tail-days (81% of all; 91% of those with 2+ legs), holding 87% of all legs. Relaxed (drop cancelled legs, then require the rest to chain): 405,622 tail-days (85%), 91% of legs.

Why chains break. Cancellations explain the largest share (and the relaxed definition handles them). The 16,754 unexplained breaks concentrate at hubs (DFW 3,552, MIA 1,987, CLT 1,706, ORD 1,356, PHL 953), where an aircraft likely flew an international or non-BTS leg in between; 5,209 of them have a gap over 4 hours. 7,318 breaks have a next departure scheduled before the previous arrival, a tail swap. Missing tails are not a cause.

Ordering. Sorting by local clock time instead of UTC reorders 6,065 tail-days (1.3%), so rotations must be ordered by UTC departure.

Ground time (preview). Between continuous operated legs, scheduled ground time has median 60 min (p05 25, p25 50, p75 77, p95 338; the long tail is overnight and long layovers). 3.0% of pairs (33,801) have negative scheduled ground time and 2.4% are under 30 minutes. See the conclusion for what this means.

What this shows. Propagation is large and builds through the day.

  • Among the 414,031 late arrivals, 59.0% carry a positive LateAircraftDelay, averaging 39.4 minutes. Across all late minutes, late-aircraft delay is the largest single cause at 46.3% (carrier 33.0%, NAS 14.6%, weather 6.0%, security 0.1%).
  • By hour: the average LateAircraftDelay per flight rises from 2.3 min for 05:00 departures to 17.0 min at 19:00 (7x). The late-arrival rate rises from 10% to 37%, and the share of late flights that are late because of the previous flight rises from 7% to ~70%.
  • By leg position: the average rises from 3.3 min on leg 1 (carry-over from the previous night) to 9.6 (leg 2), 12.4, 15.3, 20.3, 21.2 and 34.1 min on leg 7+.

This is the pattern the project is built on: early-day delays feed many downstream legs, so early legs are where buffer should be most valuable, and LateAircraftDelay provides real data to validate the simulator against. Limitation: BTS assigns it only to arrivals of 15+ minutes, so smaller knock-on delays are invisible.

7. Time handling check (local → UTC)¶

BTS times are local. The plan, implemented in bufferengine.pipeline.timeconv.scheduled_times_utc:

  1. Scheduled departure = FlightDate + CRSDepTime, localised in the origin zone (from airports_tz.csv), converted to UTC.
  2. Scheduled arrival = FlightDate + CRSArrTime in the destination zone; if that lands before the departure in UTC, roll the arrival date forward one day (overnight flights).
  3. DST: a nonexistent local time (spring-forward gap) shifts forward; an ambiguous one (fall-back hour) takes the first (daylight) occurrence.
  4. Actual times will be scheduled UTC + DepDelay / ArrDelay (BTS delays are exact minute differences), which avoids guessing the actual date.

Independent check: the UTC-derived block time must equal BTS CRSElapsedTime, which BTS computes with its own time-zone data.

8. Conclusion¶

What is usable¶

  • The base table. 1.64 million flights, no duplicates, no impossible timing values, no missing values on operated flights, and all months present. It is fit for the planned train / validation / test split.
  • Aircraft chains. 81–85% of tail-days (87–91% of legs) form clean multi-leg chains; 98.5% of consecutive operated legs connect. About 1,800 turns per day (1.11 million over 608 days) is a size an exact solver can handle day by day.
  • UTC times. Validated against an independent BTS field, so scheduled ground time can be computed.
  • Propagation signal. Strong and measurable, with a real validation target (LateAircraftDelay).

What is not usable (or only partly)¶

  • Cancelled legs (2.1%) and diverted legs (0.4%): excluded from chains; cancelled flights are scored but not evaluated.
  • ~1.5% of consecutive operated pairs are unexplained breaks (mostly at hubs, likely non-BTS legs): these tail-days are left out of the rotation table.
  • Minimum turnaround time is not in the data. It must be estimated and is an assumption.
  • International flying is absent.

Risks for later phases¶

  1. Tail numbers are as-flown, not as-planned. BTS reports the aircraft that actually flew each leg. 3.0% of continuous pairs have negative scheduled ground time (next departure scheduled before the previous arrival), which is only possible after an operational tail swap. The true planned rotation is not published, so the rotation table is a reconstruction. Swaps are partly caused by delays, so any feature built from the as-flown sequence can leak outcomes. Phase 2 must flag these turns; Phase 3 must not use as-flown chain features without checking they could be known pre-departure.
  2. Thin planned buffers. The median scheduled ground time is 60 minutes and 2.4% of turns are under 30. After subtracting an assumed MTT, many turns will have zero or negative buffer, and the optimiser needs a clearly defined per-turn maximum and treatment of turns that are already below MTT.
  3. Seasonality and distribution shift. Late-arrival rates range from 19% to 35% by month. The training window (Jan–Sep 2025) and test window (Jan–Aug 2026) both include the summer peak, but the validation window (Oct–Dec) does not, so validation is not representative of summer. Check calibration by season.
  4. Extreme delays. 0.05% of flights are over 24 hours late; the simulator needs a cap.
  5. LateAircraftDelay is censored below 15 minutes of arrival delay, so validating the simulator only on late arrivals understates small propagated delays. Validate on the aggregate and by hour/leg index rather than flight by flight.
  6. Confounding. Late-aircraft delay is higher later in the day partly because of congestion and weather, not only propagation; the simulator should be validated, not assumed causal.
  7. A real storm is in the test window. The 24–28 January 2026 event (up to 67% of flights cancelled in a day) will distort 2026 backtest averages and breaks many chains. Report the backtest with and without those days; it also offers a real-data check for the synthetic storm scenarios.
  8. External benchmark. The D1 charter's 76.43% (Cirium) will not reproduce with BTS domestic data (74.29% in 2025); present it as context only.
